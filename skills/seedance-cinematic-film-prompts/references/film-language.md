# Film Language for Seedance 2.5

Read this reference for prestige historical drama, biography, long dialogue, confession, moral argument, montage, or exposition-heavy scenes.

## The governing idea

Film pacing is not the number of cuts. It is the rate at which the audience receives meaningful new information. A slow scene can remain compelling when gaze, blocking, cost, listener response, props, or social pressure keep changing. A fast-cut scene can still feel empty when every angle repeats the same information.

## Coverage by dramatic type

### Public confrontation

Build pressure in layers:

1. Establish who is watching and why the argument is public.
2. Begin in a two-shot so the power relationship is legible.
3. Move to the speaker when the argument becomes specific.
4. Cut to the listener when a key accusation lands; keep the speaker as spatial off-screen audio.
5. Use a witness or consequential object only if it shows social cost.
6. Return to the speaker for the final phrase or threat.
7. Hold a listener reaction before the reply when the line changes the stakes.

Do not turn conflict automatically into shouting, pointing, rapid orbiting, or villainous laughter.

### Accounting, policy, or operational exposition

Let the report change what the listener understands or does. Physical evidence is one option when feasible; these are alternatives, not mandatory inserts:

- a finger moving between ledger columns;
- an abacus stopping on a total;
- a thin silver drawer;
- a nearly empty medicine drawer;
- a listener's hand pausing on a page;
- the door or crowd that represents the predicted consequence.

Keep the exact dialogue audio continuous. Visual inserts must clarify the spoken logic rather than literally illustrate every noun.

### Confession

Allow more duration on the face, but build visible thought progression:

- rehearsed or defensive opening;
- gaze loss at the difficult fact;
- contact with a meaningful prop;
- a first direct look at the listener;
- a changed sentence ending after the decision is spoken.

One restrained reaction insert can strengthen a confession. Repeated cutting to generic sympathy weakens it.

### Moral statement or biographical thesis

Earn the line through preceding material cost. Prefer a calm, specific question or decision over a slogan. The speaker may continue looking at the consequence—an empty alley, a ledger, a damaged tool—then turn toward the listener only at the final thought.

Preserve one or two genuine long takes for emotional peaks. If every dialogue scene uses the same slow push-in, none of them feels important.

## Optional emphasis move: short push-in, then hold

Use `运镜：短促推进后稳住。` selectively at a clearly established emotional turn, confrontation, consequential realization, or decision. It is an option, not a required beat, group ingredient, or default for ordinary dialogue. First ask whether the performance and existing framing already carry the moment; use the move only when closing the distance adds specific pressure, shock, or resolve.

Tie the push to an observable trigger such as standing, lifting the eyes, pointing, or the decisive spoken thought. Advance briefly, then settle into a stable composition so the performance and response can land. Leave enough time for that hold rather than continuing to rush closer or cutting immediately. Keep both the emotional subject and necessary spatial relationships readable; preserve the axis, headroom, focus, fixed lighting, and a camera route that clears furniture. The move is a camera advance, not an automatic cut or sudden digital zoom; do not add shake or lens distortion as substitutes for intensity.

Choose by dramatic progression: a slow push can support gradual accumulation; a short push followed by a hold can mark a sudden change; a fixed shot can let the aftermath carry its own weight. Avoid stacking these moves into every important line. Review neighboring scenes for repetition and prefer another expression after a recent emphasis move unless the new story beat specifically warrants it. Do not impose an every-scene quota or an absolute ban on justified repetition.

## Useful duration guidance

- Orientation: 2–5 seconds.
- Simple insert or contact event: 1–3 seconds.
- Ordinary dialogue coverage: 3–6 seconds per meaningful angle.
- Listener reaction: 2–4 seconds.
- Confrontation landing: 3–5 seconds.
- Confession hold: 8–12 seconds when performance evolves.
- More than roughly 15 seconds in one framing: use only when the long take itself creates tension and visible progression continues.

These are decision aids, not mechanical quotas.

## Continuous dialogue across cuts

When one exact source line spans several shots:

- make the full audio range one parent segment and place the angle changes inside it as `画面镜头A`, `画面镜头B`, and so on;
- print the exact line once, after all internal visual-shot instructions;
- give each internal shot an `音频状态` that points to the current time window of the single parent audio track;
- keep synchronized mouth motion only when the speaker is visible at that current time point;
- when showing the listener, prop, or witness, preserve the same voice as location-matched off-screen sound and keep the listener's mouth closed;
- never add `对白/音效` again inside an internal shot and never quote source fragments in action or performance directions;
- return to the speaker for the decisive phrase when useful, continuing from the current audio position rather than restarting.

Avoid using `同一句对白持续` as a repeated shot-level dialogue field. Models may interpret every occurrence as a new speech event and repeat the nearest semantic phrase.

## Animation and AI generation restraint

- Avoid continuous floating cameras, decorative rack focus, and unmotivated orbiting.
- A short shot should contain one readable event.
- Give hands a concrete relationship to props; avoid idle gesturing loops.
- Let wet cloth, heavy baskets, paper, doors, bodies, and furniture obey weight and contact.
- Avoid perfect symmetrical mouth motion and prolonged beauty-shot staring.
- Use reaction shots to reveal thought, not to create generic emotional wallpaper.

## Scene progression across generation groups

Design the full dramatic scene before applying generation-duration boundaries. A reporting scene may progress from joint attention to a document, to concern about consequences, to a decision expressed through action. Each group should inherit the prior ending and develop that progression rather than reset to an establishing two-shot and identical close-ups.

Use movement when a character has a reason to move: inspect stock, offer a seat, deliver medicine, or act on a decision. Do not send actors walking merely to animate dialogue. Likewise, cut to the listener while the speaker continues when the information changes the listener; do not wait automatically for the end of a line.

## Performance and feasible alternatives

Describe what a person notices and what they try to accomplish. `Hearing the water grow louder, he looks for its source and instinctively protects the basket` gives the actor a reason to react. A sequence of unrelated frowns, blinks, and lip movements does not.

When exact writing or intricate hand contact is unreliable, preserve the narrative through a medium shot, reaction, sound, or an editorial omission. If exact writing is essential, plan a prepared text reference or post-production insert rather than asking the model to invent legible characters. Respect project-specific rejections of ledger or hand close-ups without banning those shots in unrelated work.

Keep subtle acting flexible; reserve detailed micro-expression progressions for a consequential emotional turn or an explicit user request. Maintain spontaneity within the locked action and continuity.

## Interaction-led coverage

Give dialogue actors an immediate shared task when the scene supports one: present evidence, receive it, examine it, let a companion see, then return attention to the speaker. Use reciprocal reactions: the giver steadies the object, the receiver looks before taking it, a witness steps closer because they need to see. Preserve natural differences in reaction timing. Quiet listening can include a purposeful gaze or posture change; it need not become repetitive gesturing.

Choose framing by what matters at that moment. A close-up can show the decisive contact; cut as the object is withdrawn and continue in a wider shot showing the examination and the others' response. Inserts remain optional, and user-approved close-ups override a project's earlier preference to avoid them.

Keep simple shots brief once their information is legible. Allow complete exchanges, physical effort, or emotional progression enough time. Recheck cuts against the actual speech delivery: reaction images continue the same audio event, and pauses belong to the performance rather than to every new framing.

## Attention transfer in a continuous take

Define the starting subject, discovery point, reason to stop following it, and final subject. The camera may follow a moving subject, reveal a destination, then continue independently as that subject leaves. Do not force a bird, vehicle, or person into implausible movement merely to keep the camera attached. Aerial travel, ground-level observation, and a final landing need believable height, distance, and acceleration; simplify the route within the requested long take or propose separate shots for user approval if it cannot fit the duration.

Use one or two readable changes in speed tied to discovery: a calm opening, acceleration after an event, and deceleration as the new subject becomes important. Do not imply an edit at every time marker. Keep the end framing useful for the next group's first image or sound.

## Environment expressed through acting

Where visible breath is appropriate to the cold conditions, make it intermittent with speech, breathing, and exertion; wind carries it away while faces and mouths remain legible. Cold can also motivate drawing a coat closed, shifting weight, or carefully gripping a tool. Select relevant signals, not a checklist repeated for every actor. Keep skin color natural and weather behavior consistent across cuts.
